Scope

The strongest honest case against a daily morning brief (news avoidance data, interruption and checking research, Simon and Taleb on attention and noise, Knuth/Newport/Dobelli), then the chronobiology that bears on when a brief should be read. Medicine and intelligence pre-briefing is covered by another agent; only a pointer is given here.

Findings

1. News avoidance and fatigue (evidence, survey-based)

  • DNR 2024: 39% say they sometimes or often actively avoid news, up from 29% in 2017; coverage describes news as “relentless” and points to smartphones, push notifications and algorithmic feeds as possible drivers. Press Gazette. Interest in news in 12 tracked markets fell from 66% (2018) to 49% (2024) (same source).
  • DNR 2025: about 40% across 48 markets (some write-ups say 42%); among avoiders, 39% cite mood and 31% feel worn out by the amount of news. The report distinguishes consistent avoiders (low interest, low education) from selective avoiders (overloaded, protecting wellbeing by topic or time). Statista summary, Reuters Institute exec summary. The 31%/39% figures were seen only in secondary pages, not the PDF.
  • DNR 2026: avoidance reported at 42%, roughly flat on 2025; UK 50% (+4), US 45% (+3). Global trust in news 37%, a record low. Press Gazette, Advanced Television, DCU. No 2026 selective-avoider share found.
  • Caveat: these are population-wide self-reports about general news, not about a curated, technical, once-a-day digest. Selective avoidance (a protective strategy) is arguably consistent with using a curated brief.

2. Checking frequency, interruption, stress

  • Kushlev and Dunn (2015, Computers in Human Behavior 43:220-228): 124 adults, two weeks, alternating a week limited to checking email 3x/day with a week of unlimited checking. Daily stress was significantly lower in the limited week; lower stress predicted higher well-being; unlimited checkers did not feel more productive. Compliance with the limit was imperfect and participants found it hard. UBC, paper PDF. Relevance: email, not news; effect sizes described as modest. A once-daily brief is far below the 3x/day arm.
  • Mark, Voida and Cardello (CHI 2012): 13 information workers, five days without email; lower stress by heart rate variability, about half the window switching (18 vs 37 per hour); some felt isolated. UCI. Small n.
  • Mark et al. 2016 (Microsoft, about 40 employees with HRV monitors): per one blog summary, batching email was only partly supported; not better stress than checking throughout. Single secondhand source, unverified. Chris Bailey summary. This is disconfirming evidence for the simple “batch it” prescription.
  • Mark’s attention figures: screen-dwell about 2.5 minutes (2004), 75 s (2012), 47 s (2016, median 40 s); 23 min 15 s to resume an interrupted task from a mid-2000s study. Many blogs distort these; Mark herself cautions that 47 s is not a biological attention span. Self-interruption share quoted at 44% or 80% by different pages; do not use either. Texas Standard, Tools for Humans critique
  • Leroy (2009, OBHDP 109(2):168-181): two experiments; people must stop thinking about an unfinished task to transition attention fully; leaving a task unfinished produces residue and worse next-task performance. A secondary report says time pressure and closure reduced residue. The popular “40% error” figure is not in the abstract; do not use. EconPapers abstract, UW Bothell. Implication for briefs: items that open loops (unresolved news, an unanswered question) are the residue risk; a brief that closes loops (nothing actionable today) differs from one that opens them. This inference is mine.
  • Task-switching costs generally: not searched separately beyond Mark and Leroy.

3. Simon and Taleb (arguments)

  • Simon, “Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World,” in Greenberger (ed.), Computers, Communications, and the Public Interest, Johns Hopkins Press, 1971, citation pp. 40-41. Search results quote “What information consumes is rather obvious: it consumes the attention of its recipients.” and “Hence a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.” The sentence continues with a need to allocate attention efficiently (paraphrased in one result as “a need to allocate that attention efficiently among the overabundance of information sources that might consume it”). Wording seen only on blog and quote pages; the scanned PDF at atelierdesfuturs.org was not readable. Simon’s point is about filtering, which supports a curated brief as much as it opposes one.
  • Taleb, Antifragile (2012), “Noise and Signal” section: yearly observation about 1:1 signal:noise; daily about 95% noise / 5% signal; hourly about 99.5% noise / 0.5% signal. The numbers are illustrative, not empirical; one commentator notes the model assumes signal constant divided by number of intervals. Result pages also attribute to the text that anyone who listens to news (except when very, very significant events take place) is “one step below sucker”. Farnam Street, Jolly Contrarian, sive.rs. Taleb tweeted on 2019-12-27 (status ID date inferred): “Recall that the more information you get, the more disproportionately higher the ratio of noise to signal…” X. Relevance: Taleb’s claim concerns high-frequency data on slow-moving processes; a daily digest of mostly slow-moving research news is the sampling frequency he is arguing against only if the underlying signal changes yearly. The model is a stylised statistical argument (noise variance scales with sqrt of interval) from a quantitatively trained writer; it is argument, not evidence about brief-reading.
  • Knuth: stopped having an email address on 1 January 1990; his page says he is happy and explains that email suits people whose role is to be on top of things while his is to be on the bottom of things. Knuth Stanford page, Wikiquote. Anecdote, but from the most credentialed practitioner in the field; he relies on a secretary to triage mail, i.e., he outsourced the brief.
  • Newport: attention residue concept borrowed from Leroy in Deep Work (2016); news argument in Digital Minimalism (2019). No verified verbatim news quote found; a widely circulated “manic information addicts” line is only on a quote site (WEAK; do not use). TED Ideas excerpt
  • Dobelli, “Avoid News: Towards a Healthy News Diet” (essay dated 2010 on the PDF; Guardian extract April 2013; book Stop Reading the News). Contains “news is to the mind what sugar is to the body” (per PDF coverage). Secondary posts list “News misleads us systematically” and “News is irrelevant”. Taleb publicly accused Dobelli of plagiarism in 2019 per Wikipedia; treat Dobelli as secondary and not scientifically credentialed. Dobelli PDF at Gwern, Wikipedia

4. Chronobiology and the first hour

  • Sleep inertia: temporary sleepiness, disorientation and impaired cognition after waking; performance decrements dissipate with time awake and are exaggerated by prior sleep loss and the time of day of waking (Hilditch and McHill 2019). Cerebral blood flow velocity below pre-sleep levels for up to about 30 minutes in the review’s brain-activity section; an EASA-commissioned review examined whether the conventional 30 minutes is valid but I did not see its verdict; individual variability is high. Dove Press, PMC, EASA. Implication: the first 30 or so minutes are poor for judgment either way; skim-reading is more tolerant than analysis, but I found no study on reading specifically.
  • Synchrony effect: performance, especially anything needing inhibition of distraction, is better at one’s circadian peak; the effect is strongest in older adults and morning types, and absent in younger neutral types in a 2017 study. May and Hasher 1998, APA Monitor, Schmidt et al. 2007.
  • Wieth and Zacks (2011, Thinking and Reasoning 17(4):387-401): insight problem solving was better at non-optimal times of day; no consistent time-of-day effect on analytic problems. Mechanism (reduced inhibitory control broadens attention) is the authors’ hypothesis. Neutral-type participants showed no difference. Sample 428 students, subgroups small. BPS Digest, abstract. Caution: this does not support “do analytic work whenever”, and the analytic null conflicts with the common claim that analytic work peaks at the chronotype peak; the evidence is mixed.
  • Morning phone checking: no direct experiment found on checking on waking. A PNAS Nexus (Feb 2025) experiment blocking mobile internet reportedly improved sustained attention and wellbeing, seen only via blog summary and not specific to mornings; cortisol and “80% check within 15 minutes” figures are unverified commentary. Psychology Today
  • Implication (inference, not a finding): put deep work in your personal peak window, read the brief afterward or at a natural break, in a bounded format with an end; avoid reading it in the sleep-inertia window if it needs judgment; schedule it before deep work only if it clears specific decisions (closes loops) for that day.

5. Counter-evidence (pointer)

Pre-operative team briefings: observational and before-after support for fewer communication failures; no RCT of briefings found; checklist meta-analysis (2014 Anesthesiology, seven nonrandomized studies, 37,339 patients) found fewer complications. PubMed 18209148, Anesthesiology PDF. Another agent covers this and intelligence briefing. Note these briefings are team-coordination tools, not individual news digests.

Disagreements, failure modes and caveats

  • Strongest evidence for abstention concerns email, push notifications and general mass-market news; none tests a single curated daily technical digest.
  • Batching is not clearly better: the 2016 Mark study (secondhand) found batchers no less stressed; Kushlev participants struggled to comply.
  • Taleb’s noise numbers are illustrative; 1:1 yearly signal is assumed, and a brief on research news with weekly or monthly structural change has a different ratio. Taleb also exempts “very significant events”, which is the job of an alert, not a digest.
  • Simon’s actual prescription is filtering; a brief is a filter. The quote is routinely used against its own author’s design implication.
  • Wieth and Zacks and the synchrony literature disagree on analytic work; the chronotype effect is weak or absent in young neutral types, so “never read before deep work” is not established.
  • Sleep inertia: durations uncertain; the 30-minute convention is under review.
  • Survey avoidance figures differ by year/source (40 vs 42% for 2025); self-report; “avoidance” includes people who are simply uninterested.
  • Quote hygiene: Newport “manic information addicts” and 23-minute and 40% figures circulate in low-quality sources.
  • Dobelli’s credentials are not scientific; plagiarism dispute with Taleb.

Net-positive vs net-negative conditions (for deep technical work)

Likely net-positive when:

  1. The brief is bounded (fixed length, once a day, with a clear end) and replaces ad hoc checking of feeds rather than adding to it.
  2. It is read at a defined time, after the first deep block or at a transition, not in the sleep-inertia window.
  3. It closes loops: filtered to what changes decisions today, with explicit “nothing to act on” states, to limit attention residue.
  4. Its domain moves fast enough that a day’s delay costs something (security advisories, dependencies, releases).
  5. It is the filter Simon calls for, i.e., it reduces total information intake.
  6. The reader would otherwise check sources compulsively (the brief is a substitute). Likely net-negative when:
  7. It is additive to feeds, email and chat, raising checking frequency.
  8. It is read first thing, before one’s best focus window, on a reader who has a strong peak early.
  9. Content is mostly general news, outrage or slow-moving stories, giving Taleb’s low-signal sampling.
  10. It opens loops (unresolved issues, to-dos) just before deep work.
  11. Reading it becomes a ritual regardless of content, with no skip rule when nothing changed.
  12. The reader is already in a news-fatigue state (mood effects, worn out).
