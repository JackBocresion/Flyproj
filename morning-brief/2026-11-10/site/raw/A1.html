Findings

Intelligence: estimative language

  • Kent’s 1964 essay “Words of Estimative Probability” (Studies in Intelligence, Fall 1964) mapped words to odds. Midpoints reported consistently: almost certain 93% (±6), probable 75% (±12), chances about even 50% (±10), probably not 30% (±10), almost certainly not 7% (±5) (GlobalSecurity; Wikipedia). Opinion/history, not a trial: these were Kent’s ranges, based on colleagues’ readings.
  • Origin story: NIE 29-51 (March 1951), “Probability of an Invasion of Yugoslavia in 1951”, said an attack was a “serious possibility”. Kent says he meant about 65:35 for an attack; colleagues read it as much lower, with reported spreads of ~20-80% (secondary, unconfirmed against primary) (Wikipedia; Kesselman thesis).
  • Kent split colleagues into “poets” (prefer words) and “mathematicians” (prefer numbers) (Wikipedia).
  • Modern practice: ICD 203 prescribes seven likelihood bands (e.g. almost no chance/remote 01-05%, very unlikely 05-20%, unlikely 20-45%, roughly even 45-55%, likely 55-80%, almost certain 95-99%) and forbids combining a confidence level and a likelihood term in one sentence (ICD 203 PDF; table as reproduced in GitHub copy). Table values come from a secondary reproduction; check against the PDF.
  • Evidence on perception: a 2023 survey of 123 people found perceptions of these words largely consistent with Kent’s era, with wide spreads (arXiv 2405.15185 via search summary). Wording remains understood differently by different readers, so a numeric range is safer.
  • Evidence on accuracy: Mandel and Barnes (PNAS 2014) assessed 1,514 strategic intelligence forecasts from a Canadian unit (2005-2011); discrimination and calibration were “very good”, better for senior analysts and easier questions; analysts were more underconfident on high-importance questions (PMC; Phys.org).

Intelligence: PDB format

  • BLUF structure: the what, the why, what comes next; typically one page (Cipher Brief, Becky Root). Early PDBs were very short: Brennan said the first issue was 14 items, most no more than two sentences (CIA, Brennan keynote).
  • Live briefer: Priess (former daily briefer under Clinton and Bush) argues the briefer’s value is answering questions and discussing nuance, not late updates (reported in a search summary; exact wording could not be confirmed) (Lawfare; Stratfor). Context only: Bush reportedly valued candid “I don’t know; I’ll get it for you” answers from briefers (secondary).
  • Meador and Cerf (Studies in Intelligence, Dec 2013) proposed a tablet PDB with the briefer “driving” interactive content (CIA; Nextgov). Proposal, not evidence of effect.
  • Helgerson, Getting to Know the President (CSI 1996): covers briefings of candidates and presidents-elect, based on declassified records and interviews; as Clinton’s briefer he reportedly avoided trying to influence Clinton’s views. No findings retrieved (CSI/GovInfo listing).

Intelligence: critiques and failures

  • Tyranny of current intelligence: a CIA analytic-pathologies report has a section so titled, calling it “a plague recognized by all informed critics” per the search summary (CIA PDF). A 2016 Air University paper and a 2010 Intelligence and National Security article make the same claim for the US and UK (JCS paper; T&F). Opinion/analysis, not measurement.
  • August 6 2001 PDB: titled “Bin Ladin Determined to Strike in US”; prepared in response to the president’s questions; per the Commission, the 36th PDB item that year on Bin Ladin/al Qaeda and the first devoted to a US attack. The White House fact sheet says it was mostly historical analysis, with no mention of planes as weapons (Wikipedia; White House fact sheet; Commission ch. 8). The chapter is titled “The System Was Blinking Red”, from Tenet. The lesson drawn depends on who reads it: a historical item delivered into a flood of 36 items, versus a missed alarm. Contested.
  • Politicization/pressure: Morell, a briefer to Bush, is reported to call Libby’s interventions “the most blatant attempt to politicize intelligence that I saw in thirty three years” (reviewer’s paraphrase/quote, unverified), yet in a 2015 NPR interview called the pressure-on-Iraq thesis “a myth” (NPR). The 2005 WMD Commission said tough policymaker questioning is not politicization (report). Kerr’s 2004 review warned of analysts giving more certainty than the facts supported (secondary, GMU chapter).
  • Morell put his confidence in the bin Laden compound at about 60% (Army Times/AP). Illustrates stating confidence to the reader.

Medicine: morning report

  • Multicentre survey: residents report high attendance, clinical duties are the main barrier (85%); residents rate clinical reasoning (82%) and evidence-based medicine (77%) as top topics (JGIM 2020). Program director presence linked to higher attendance (78% vs 61%) (PMC8477724). Older survey: most programs lack written goals and 77% do not track outcomes (Validation review). A resident-as-teacher intervention improved meeting audience needs, d=0.26 (PMC1435882). Evidence is perception surveys; no patient-outcome data found.

Medicine: handoffs

  • I-PASS (Starmer et al., NEJM 2014): nine hospitals, 10,740 admissions; medical errors 24.5 to 18.8 per 100 admissions (23% relative reduction); preventable adverse events 4.7 to 3.3 (30%); nonpreventable events unchanged; oral handoff time unchanged, 2.4 vs 2.5 min (NEJM; ScienceDaily). Design: prospective pre-post, no randomization or concurrent control, authors state it precludes establishing causation; reductions seen at six of nine sites (MHS IV review). A PSNet page mislabels 23% as preventable adverse events.
  • Joint Commission: “about 80%” of serious errors involving miscommunication at handoff traces to one spokesperson; other estimates 50-80% or over 75%. In its Hand-off Communications Project receivers judged about 37% of handoffs unsuccessful and full implementers cut defective handoffs by 52% on average (Healthcare IT News; Sentinel Event Alert 58). Treat 80% as folklore.

Medicine: surgical checklists and briefings

  • Haynes et al. (NEJM 2009): 8 hospitals in 8 countries, 7,688 patients (3,733 before, 3,955 after); death 1.5% to 0.8% (P=0.003); inpatient complications 11.0% to 7.0% (P<0.001); 19-item checklist; before/after design (NEJM; Harvard Gazette).
  • Urbach et al. (NEJM 2014), Ontario: 101 hospitals, 109,341 procedures before and 106,370 after; adjusted mortality 0.71% to 0.65% (OR 0.91, P=0.13); no significant complication change (NEJM; ICES). Compliance was not measured. Critics (including Gawande) say a mandate without training, culture work or compliance monitoring explains the null; a blogger calculated about 40% power (not Gawande’s) (Incidental Economist; Medscape; KevinMD).
  • Reames et al. 2015, Michigan Medicare: difference-in-differences against 950 non-participating hospitals; 30-day mortality RR 1.03 (0.97-1.10); $516 higher index-admission payments (Medical Care abstract). Second null result in a real-world rollout.
  • Lingard et al. (Arch Surg 2008): 302 procedures; checklist-structured team briefing reduced communication failures per procedure (one source: three-fold) (PubMed; PSNet). An earlier paper reports paradoxical effects of briefings on OR performance (Springer).
  • Gawande’s team-introduction step: reported as having felt “hokey” to him, justified by research that people who know names work together better (HuffPost review, secondary) (HuffPost). The WHO form says it “is not intended to be comprehensive” (WHO form). Pronovost central-line checklist: Keystone linked to about 10% fewer deaths, which researchers say they could not attribute definitively (JHU Gazette).

Military briefings

  • Bumiller, NYT 27 Apr 2010 “We Have Met the Enemy and He Is PowerPoint”: Gen. Mattis said “PowerPoint makes us stupid”; McMaster (banned PowerPoint at Tal Afar 2005) said it can create “the illusion of understanding and the illusion of control” and that “some problems in the world are not bullet-izable”. The article also describes McChrystal reacting to the “spaghetti” COIN-dynamics slide. Some sites misattribute the Mattis line to McMaster (article PDF repost; Nextgov). Critique is anecdotal; no outcome data.

Aviation CRM

  • CRM training started in 1979 after high-profile accidents. Meta-analyses find effects mostly at attitude and learning level; about half of studies in one 2006 review mixed; a healthcare umbrella review found outcome evidence unsupported (O’Connor meta-analysis; Umbrella review). Briefing-specific evidence not found.

Trading desks

  • No usable evidence found on morning meetings or research morning notes. Only generic: buy-side reports are typically one to two pages (Wikipedia).

Disagreements, failure modes and caveats

  • Strong designs are rare. I-PASS and Haynes are before/after; Ontario and Michigan, run as real rollouts, were null. The best defensible summary: structured communication helps when implemented with training and measured compliance; mandated or ritual use may not.
  • The Ontario null is itself contested (power, 3-month windows, no compliance data). Gawande’s rebuttal is an inference.
  • I-PASS did not lengthen the oral handoff (2.4 vs 2.5 min), a useful design lesson: structure without more time.
  • Words vs numbers: Kent’s table versus ICD 203 bands differ in numbers; readers vary. Kent himself used a numeric “65:35” private reading for “serious possibility” versus colleagues’ much lower readings.
  • Format versus content: the PDB 36-items-before-Aug-6 story cuts both ways: volume of items can numb readers; a historical piece may fail to stand out.
  • Politicization: a briefer’s own accounts conflict (Morell on Libby versus “a myth”). Do not treat a single memoir line as the consensus.
  • The “80% of errors” handoff figure is a single spokesperson’s estimate.
  • CRM evidence is mostly attitudes and training, not briefing formats.
  • PowerPoint critique is quote-driven; no measurement of decision quality.
