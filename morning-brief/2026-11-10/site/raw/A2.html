Scope

First-hand accounts and evidence on how mathematicians, physicists, computer scientists and programmers take in information each day (arXiv listings, email, feeds, LLM summaries), and on mornings. Includes the Haque and Ginsparg arXiv position-effect papers as evidence that ordering in a daily brief steers expert attention. Covers both camps: people who want an overnight research agent, and people (Knuth, Graham, Newport) who protect themselves from the stream.

Findings

1. arXiv listing position as a de facto brief-ordering experiment (strongest evidence in the slice)

  • Haque and Ginsparg, “Positional effects on citation and readership in arXiv”, JASIST 60(11):2203-2218, 2009 (Wiley, arXiv:0907.4740). Data: articles deposited 2002-2004 in astro-ph, hep-th, hep-ph (Scholarly Kitchen, 2009-07-29).
  • Abstract numbers as relayed by search: articles in position 1 had median citations 83% (astro-ph), 50% (hep-th) and 100% (hep-ph) higher than those lower down. Early full-text downloads were higher by 82%, 61% and 58% respectively (Scholarly Kitchen; arXiv:0907.4740). The preprint (v1) gives raw astro-ph medians for position 1 versus positions 10-40 (one search snippet said 10 vs 4; another said about 89.8 vs 44.6; versions and metric differ, unresolved).
  • Mechanism: the authors attribute the correlation “primarily” to authors intentionally self-promoting (timing submission to land first). They also isolate a pure visibility effect among papers that landed early by accident: boosts of 44%, 38%, 71% (astro-ph, hep-th, hep-ph) (arXiv:0907.4740). So ordering does steer readership, independent of paper quality, though most of the raw gap is self-selection.
  • Sequel: “Last but not Least” (arXiv:1010.2757, JASIST 61, Dec 2010) finds “reverse-visibility” at the ends of listings and a “procrastination” effect: last-position papers submitted just before the deadline are cited more than those that landed there by chance, for one subcommunity (arXiv). The paper notes some users suggested randomising order, per user or for all; I found no evidence arXiv did this (arXiv HTML). Also notes that astro-ph was later subdivided so effects may differ.
  • Earlier related paper: Dietrich, “Disentangling visibility and self-promotion bias in the arXiv:astro-ph positional citation effect” (arXiv:0805.0307) and Dietrich 2008 PASP/IOP “The Importance of Being First” (IOP).
  • Disconfirming/qualifying: Motloch’s informal blog analysis (hep-th 2017-2020; not peer reviewed) finds first-listing papers are more cited but that restricting to papers submitted in the first minute largely removes the position effect, concluding the advantage reflects who submits right after the deadline more than position itself (Motloch, GitHub). Deadline changed on 2017-01-02 (same source). Caution: this suggests the 2009 “visibility” estimate may be inflated; treat position steering as real but modest.
  • Readers’ habit: arXiv offers daily email, RSS, a /new page and /recent (last five days, newest day first) (arXiv math new, arXiv HTML 1010.2757). A math grad-student blog describes spending a minute or two scanning titles daily, with over 32,000 new math submissions in 2018 (AMS blog, 2019-10-03). Anecdote, not a survey.
  • Volume context (2026): arXiv received 40,363 papers in September 2026, more than double two years earlier, per one outlet citing arXiv; arXiv imposed a limit of two submissions per calendar month and three active submissions from 2026-10-01 (arXiv blog, Socket). Evidence: an organisation announcement plus press.

2. Terence Tao

  • Daily monitoring: in his 2026-10-01 post on arXiv’s rate limits he says he can “mostly monitor the arXiv each night” to learn whether someone has solved a problem he is working on, “imperfect, but … enormously useful” (Tao blog). He worries about priority disputes when submissions wait for slots and expects private circulation to grow.
  • AI as literature tool: Mathstodon: AI literature search “already well established” as a way to gain initial insight or connect a technique to the literature (Mathstodon as surfaced by search; wording is relayed, verify). His “AI views — a living summary” page says AI is safer as an accelerated search engine (further literature, definitions of unfamiliar terms) than as a summariser of papers you cannot yet read unaided; and the page discloses it was drafted by Claude from his public writing and reviewed by him (Tao web). This is a direct “summaries vs primary sources” position from a leading mathematician, and a pro-agent one with guardrails.
  • Escalation: a March 2026 write-up reports he said models are “ready for primetime” in math and theoretical physics, using AI for literature search, code, plots, calculations (OpenAI Academy, 2026-03-06; an OpenAI-hosted page, so promotional context). In Sept 2024 he compared o1 to advising “a mediocre, but not completely incompetent, graduate student” and later said this was not an equivalence claim (Simon Willison feed via search; the model was o1, not GPT-4).
  • Caution: his Mathstodon posts note AI is depleting easy open problems and that published solutions can contaminate later reasoning; literature signal-to-noise drops (secondary summary via Decrypt).
  • Not found: any Tao post on how he reads papers or his morning routine.

3. Knuth (the anti-“on top of things” camp)

  • Stopped email 1 January 1990 after about 15 years; his Stanford page “Email (let’s drop the hyphen)” (Knuth). Wikiquote/AZQuotes relay the line about being “on the bottom of things” (Wikiquote); my page-targeted search did not show that sentence in a snippet from the page itself.
  • Mechanism of his workaround: his secretary prints non-spam messages sent to taocp@ and knuth-bug@ and he replies in writing; he aims to reply within six months (per help page). Newport stresses the executive-assistant filter that makes this possible (Chronicle, 2019-02-13). So Knuth’s position is “delegated, batched, delayed”, not “disconnected”.
  • The “once every three months” claim traces to Brian Christian’s book via secondary pages; unverified against Knuth (Cal Newport bonus post).
  • Counterpoint within the same person: Knuth’s “Claude’s Cycles” (28 Feb 2026, revised 14 Apr 2026) credits Claude Opus 4.6 with an odd-m construction for an open Hamiltonian-decomposition problem after about 31 guided explorations over about an hour (Filip Stappers ran it); Knuth wrote the proof; the even case remained open at that time (Knuth PDF, Adafruit). So he rejects the inbox but not AI as a collaborator on a problem he chose.

9. Curated digests, alerts and summaries vs primary sources

  • Practitioner guides: alerts are enthusiastically set up and abandoned within a month due to overlapping emails (Paper Digest guide, vendor, so interested); a PhD student scanned 100-200 RSS items a day and downloaded a handful (THE); Science career column on combining RSS, Scholar, colleagues (Science). Anecdotal; no survey found.
  • Evidence on LLM summaries: Royal Society Open Science study (about 5,000 summaries of 200 abstracts and 100 full articles): newer chatbots were up to 9x (ChatGPT-4o) and 39x (Llama 3.3) more likely than originals to overgeneralise conclusions; asking for accuracy made it about twice worse (Inside Higher Ed, 2025-04-24). Fabricated-reference rate for GPT-4 about 18% in one review (older model; PMC). Think-aloud study: 10 of 15 researchers limited AI to low-stakes tasks (arXiv:2604.23136).
  • Adoption: share of arXiv math submissions with confirmed AI use rose from 4.75% in March to 24.14% through Aug 20 (2026), per a paper surfaced by search (arXiv:2608.24961, unverified detail). Gowers (2026-08-12) argues models excel at combining known methods (Gowers).

Disagreements, failure modes and caveats

  • Self-promotion versus position: Haque/Ginsparg say position effect is mostly authors gaming the slot, with a smaller pure visibility share (44/38/71%); Motloch argues position adds little once submission time is controlled. For a brief, ordering plausibly steers attention, but the size of the causal effect is contested.
  • Knuth is not disconnected: delegation to a secretary and six-month reply windows. Newport makes this point. “Not on top of things” is a filtered, batched channel, closer to a human overnight agent than to nothing.
  • Graham’s own disconnection strategy failed over time (his note to “Disconnecting Distraction”).
  • Tao: pro-AI but warns against outsourcing reading you cannot check, and notes literature contamination and depletion of open problems.
  • LLM summaries overgeneralise (up to 9x to 39x in newer models in one study) and prompting for accuracy backfired; fabricated references persist. Pro-agent camp counters with Karpathy’s manual-first pass and Tao’s verification stance.
  • Alert fatigue: overlapping alerts get abandoned.
  • Source quality: the Tao “living summary” was drafted by Claude; the OpenAI Academy piece is vendor-hosted; Wolfram night-owl claim is a commenter’s; Currey inclusion of Poincare unconfirmed.
